# MediHealth Clinic — Phishing Simulation Plan

## 1. Purpose

Evaluate whether employees can recognise, verify, and report a simulated phishing email.

The exercise will identify learning needs and opportunities to improve the reporting process.

This is a proposed exercise for a fictional clinic. No simulation has been conducted.

## 2. Scope and Audience

The proposed campaign covers all 50 employees, including medical, reception, billing, and administrative personnel.

Messages should be relevant to each group’s work. Delivery should accommodate shifts and avoid periods of heavy clinical activity.

## 3. Approval and Responsibilities

Before implementation, obtain documented approval for the audience, timing, message, platform, data collection, and stop conditions.

| Proposed role | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| Clinic management | Approve the exercise and its boundaries. |
| IT lead | Configure and test the simulation and reporting process. |
| Training coordinator | Prepare the learning material and debrief. |
| Clinical operations lead | Review timing and potential effects on care. |
| Designated privacy lead | Review data collection, access, and retention arrangements. |

These roles require confirmation; the scenario does not establish that each role already exists.

## 4. Simulation Scenario

Use a fictional insurance account update based on `sample-phishing-email.md`.

Adapt the message so it:

- Contains recognisable warning signs.
- Uses fictional information.
- Does not refer to an actual patient.
- Does not request passwords, verification codes, payments, or downloads.
- Directs clicks to an approved educational landing page.

The `.example` addresses in the portfolio sample are illustrative. A real exercise would require an approved simulation platform and controlled destination.

## 5. Educational Landing Page

The landing page should explain:

“This was an approved learning exercise. No password is required. Let’s review the warning signs together.”

Include:

- The message’s warning signs.
- How to verify an unexpected request.
- The clinic’s reporting process.
- What to do after an accidental interaction.

Do not include a credential entry form.

## 6. Proposed Exercise Sequence

### Phase 1 — Preparation

- Confirm written approval and responsibilities.
- Define the reporting route.
- Inform employees that periodic awareness exercises form part of the programme.
- Test the message and landing page with approved test accounts.
- Confirm that tracking distinguishes employee actions from automated email scanning where possible.
- Establish a process for questions and unexpected effects.

### Phase 2 — Baseline Exercise

If approved, conduct an initial exercise before training to establish a baseline.

Record the campaign conditions, including audience, message difficulty, and delivery window.

### Phase 3 — Training

Deliver the session described in `training-outline.md`.

Explain warning signs, independent verification, reporting, and response after accidental interaction.

### Phase 4 — Follow-Up Exercise

Propose a follow-up exercise two to four weeks after training.

Use a different message with comparable difficulty and the same measurement method.

### Phase 5 — Debrief and Review

- Share aggregate results.
- Explain the warning signs.
- Recognise prompt reporting.
- Offer supportive follow-up learning.
- Identify improvements to reporting and technical controls.

## 7. Measurement Definitions

Use successfully delivered messages as the denominator. Record bounced or undelivered messages separately.

| Metric | Calculation or definition |
|---|---|
| Reporting rate | Unique recipients who report the simulation ÷ recipients with successfully delivered messages × 100 |
| Confirmed click rate | Unique recipients with a confirmed human click ÷ recipients with successfully delivered messages × 100 |
| Reporting time | Time between delivery and the recipient’s first report |
| Knowledge-check pass rate | Participants meeting the agreed pass mark ÷ participants completing the check × 100 |

A recipient may both click and report, so these categories can overlap.

Email security scanners can automatically open links. Validate tracking before treating a recorded click as an employee action.

Do not use email open rate as the main measure of success because tracking can be unreliable.

## 8. Assessing Improvement

Compare baseline and follow-up results for:

- Increased reporting rate.
- Reduced confirmed click rate.
- Faster reporting.
- Better knowledge-check performance.

Report changes in percentage points when comparing rates.

For example, an increase from 20% to 35% is a 15-percentage-point increase. These numbers are illustrative, not campaign results.

Consider differences in message difficulty, staffing, delivery, and tracking before attributing improvement to training.

A single exercise does not prove that phishing risk has been eliminated.

## 9. Data Handling and Supportive Use

- Collect only information needed to evaluate and support learning.
- Restrict identifiable results to approved personnel.
- Use aggregate results in management summaries.
- Do not publish employee names or identifiable campaign results on GitHub.
- Agree a retention period before the exercise.
- Avoid public rankings or shaming.
- Use results to improve training and processes.

For this public portfolio, include only fictional examples and clearly labelled illustrative results.

## 10. Stop Conditions

Pause the exercise if:

- It interferes with patient care or clinic operations.
- The landing page or tracking behaves unexpectedly.
- Sensitive information is submitted unintentionally.
- A real security incident requires attention.
- Management withdraws approval.

Report unexpected effects through the agreed escalation route.
