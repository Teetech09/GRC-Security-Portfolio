# MediHealth Clinic — Phishing Awareness Training Outline

## 1. Training Purpose

Help employees recognise suspicious emails, verify unexpected requests, and report possible phishing promptly.

The training connects safe email habits to protecting patient information and keeping clinic services running.

## 2. Audience and Format

**Audience:** All 50 employees, with examples relevant to medical, reception, billing, and administrative staff.

**Duration:** 30 minutes.

**Format:** Short presentation, group discussion, and a practical email review.

**Delivery:** Repeat the session in small groups to accommodate shifts and maintain patient services.

## 3. Learning Objectives

By the end of the session, employees should be able to:

- Identify at least three phishing warning signs.
- Check a sender’s full email address and inspect links safely.
- Explain why email authentication results are supporting checks rather than proof of safety.
- Verify an unexpected request through a trusted channel.
- Report suspicious messages and accidental interactions promptly.

## 4. Session Plan

| Time | Topic | Activity |
|---|---|---|
| 3 minutes | Why phishing matters in healthcare | Discuss how stolen accounts or unavailable records could affect clinic work. |
| 5 minutes | Common phishing tactics | Explain impersonation, urgency, unexpected attachments, and requests for credentials. |
| 7 minutes | Review the sample email | Ask staff to identify warning signs in the fictional insurance message. |
| 5 minutes | Check and verify | Demonstrate sender inspection, safe link inspection, and independent verification. |
| 5 minutes | Report and respond | Explain the reporting process and what to do after an accidental interaction. |
| 5 minutes | Knowledge check and questions | Use short scenarios to check understanding. |

## 5. Suggested Presentation Slides

### Slide 1 — Protecting Patients Starts With Everyday Actions

Key message:

A careful response to an unexpected email can help protect patient information and keep clinic services available.

Explain that phishing can target anyone, including experienced staff.

### Slide 2 — What Is Phishing?

Phishing is a deceptive message designed to persuade someone to reveal information, open harmful content, or take an unsafe action.

Healthcare examples include:

- A fake patient document notification.
- An unexpected insurance account update.
- A false request from the clinic manager.
- A fake IT message asking for a password reset.

### Slide 3 — Warning Signs to Notice

Look for:

- Unexpected requests.
- Pressure to act immediately.
- Requests for passwords or verification codes.
- Unfamiliar or misleading addresses.
- Unexpected links and attachments.
- Instructions that discourage checking with someone else.

A polished message can still be fraudulent. Poor spelling is not required for phishing.

### Slide 4 — Let’s Review an Email Together

Use the fictional email in `sample-phishing-email.md`.

Ask:

1. What is the sender asking you to do?
2. Which details make you pause?
3. How would you verify the request?
4. How would you report it?

Explain the warning signs after staff share their answers.

### Slide 5 — Check the Sender and Verify the Request

- Expand the sender details to see the full address.
- Check the address against a known legitimate source.
- Inspect a link destination without opening it where your device supports this.
- Open a trusted portal independently instead of using an unexpected email link.
- Contact the organisation using a number or address you already trust.

Do not use contact details supplied only in the suspicious message to verify that same message.

### Slide 6 — Optional Check: Email Authentication

Explain that some email applications allow staff to view technical authentication results.

- SPF checks whether a server is authorised for the technical sending domain.
- DKIM checks a digital signature on signed parts of the message.
- DMARC checks domain alignment with the visible From address.

Use the application-specific instructions in `sample-phishing-email.md`.

Key message:

PASS does not guarantee safety. FAIL or unclear results can be reported to IT for review. Staff do not need to interpret headers before reporting a suspicious message.

### Slide 7 — Pause, Check, Verify, Report

**Pause:** Do not rush because a message sounds urgent.

**Check:** Review the sender, request, and links.

**Verify:** Use a trusted contact or independently opened portal.

**Report:** Follow the clinic’s approved reporting process.

If a patient-related request is urgent, verify it promptly through the normal clinical or administrative channel.

### Slide 8 — What If You Already Clicked?

Report what happened promptly.

Tell IT whether you:

- Clicked a link.
- Entered a password or verification code.
- Downloaded or opened a file.
- Approved an unexpected sign-in request.

Follow IT instructions. Do not assume that clicking alone proves compromise.

The purpose of reporting is to protect people and systems, not to blame employees.

### Slide 9 — Quick Knowledge Check

Use the questions below and discuss the answers.

### Slide 10 — Your Reporting Route

Before delivering the session, replace these placeholders:

- **Reporting method:** [Approved report button, email address, or helpdesk process]
- **Urgent contact:** [Verified IT telephone number]

## 6. Knowledge Check

### Question 1

An insurance email says your account will be suspended in one hour unless you sign in through its link. What should you do?

**Expected answer:** Pause, verify through a known contact or trusted portal, and report the message if suspicious.

### Question 2

An email passes SPF, DKIM, and DMARC. Does this prove the request is safe?

**Expected answer:** No. Authentication checks do not establish that the sender’s request is trustworthy.

### Question 3

You entered your password into an unexpected website. What should you do next?

**Expected answer:** Contact IT immediately through a trusted channel and explain what happened.

### Question 4

A message uses your manager’s name but asks for an unusual action. How should you verify it?

**Expected answer:** Contact the manager through an established channel rather than relying on the message’s reply address.

## 7. Training Evaluation

Proposed measures:

- Attendance by department and shift.
- Percentage of participants answering at least three of four questions correctly.
- Percentage able to describe the reporting process.
- Participant feedback on clarity and confidence.
- Follow-up simulation results, assessed separately.

These are planned measures. No training results have been collected.

A short pre-session check can be compared with the post-session check to assess changes in understanding.

## 8. Reinforcement Plan

Proposed activities:

- Include phishing awareness in new employee induction.
- Share a short monthly reminder.
- Provide quarterly refresher discussions.
- Update examples after relevant incidents or changes in threats.
- Review results and provide supportive follow-up training.

Management should agree the schedule and assign an owner.

## 9. Preparation Checklist

Before delivering the session:

- Confirm the reporting route and contact details.
- Adapt demonstrations to the clinic’s email application.
- Use fictional examples without real patient information.
- Arrange sessions around clinical responsibilities.
- Prepare accessible materials and time for questions.
- Confirm how attendance and learning results will be recorded.
